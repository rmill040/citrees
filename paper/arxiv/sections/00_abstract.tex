Conditional inference trees and forests choose a split variable by an
association test before searching for a threshold, which removes the preference
of CART-style splitting for features with many cutpoints. We measure whether
that correction improves a feature ranking used for downstream prediction, and
what it costs. Conditional inference trees (CIT) and forests (CIF) are
benchmarked against 15 classification and 16 regression selectors, among them
partykit's ctree and cforest, random forests, gradient boosting, and permutation
and wrapper methods, on 31 real datasets, scoring each ranking by the held-out
accuracy of fixed learners fitted to its top-$k$ features, with synthetic
designs where the informative features are known. CIF ranks tied 3rd of 17 in
classification and 2nd of 18 in regression, ahead of cforest and ctree, level
with random forests, and behind recursive feature elimination and XGBoost;
single trees rank near the bottom. The cost has one source: the per-threshold
Bonferroni test needs a permutation budget of order $K^2/\alpha$ in the number
of candidate thresholds $K$, and the ablations place the fitting time there
rather than in the feature test. A max-type threshold test resolves the same
level at $999K$ evaluations, is calibrated closer to the nominal level, and loses
0.006 in power at matched size (95 percent interval over cells $-0.011$ to
$-0.002$), moving the forest to 5th and 4th. The adaptive stopping rule has
null size 0.0488 at 999 permutations. For a practitioner, CIF gives a ranking
level with random forests that trades CART's high-cardinality preference for a
low-cardinality one, at a fitting cost about two orders of magnitude above a
random forest's under the per-threshold rule.
