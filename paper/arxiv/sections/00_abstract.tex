Trees that choose a feature and threshold in one search favor features with
many possible thresholds; conditional inference trees (CIT) and forests (CIF)
test features first to reduce this bias, but their repeated permutation tests
make them costly. We benchmark CIT and CIF as feature rankers against 15
classification and 16 regression rankers on 31 real datasets and synthetic
designs with known informative features, scoring each top-$k$ list by the
held-out balanced accuracy or $R^2$ of fixed learners. CIF is competitive
with random forests (RF), tied 3rd of 17 and 2nd of 18, and ranks ahead of every
other conditional inference method, while permutation-based importance ranks
below RF. On the panels complete for every method, learner, and list size it is
tied 6th and tied 4th, and the regression ordering is descriptive. Its recovery
of informative features is no better than the middle of the field. The ranking rests on the forest; unbiased split selection removes
the RF preference for many-valued features without improving recovery or
prediction in our designs. The split tests set the cost: at the minimum budget their Bonferroni
corrections cost of order $m^2/\alpha$ and $K^2/\alpha$ permutations per node
for $m$ features and $K$ thresholds. A max-type threshold test keeps the
fixed-node guarantee with about $999K$ statistic evaluations, loses 0.006 in
power at equal realized size, and moves CIF to 5th and 4th. CIF thus competes
with RF as a feature ranker while splitting only when a permutation test
rejects; those tests are valid at a fixed node in trees grown on all rows, and
the max-type test makes them affordable.
