Conditional inference trees (CIT) and conditional inference forests (CIF)
reduce split-selection bias by testing features before choosing split
thresholds, but repeated permutation tests and threshold searches can make these
methods computationally expensive. We study CIT and CIF as top-$k$
feature-ranking methods for downstream prediction using real-data benchmarks,
runtime ablations, and synthetic feature-recovery experiments.
At a fixed node, if the features and permutation budget do not depend on the
node responses, Bonferroni-corrected $+1$ Monte Carlo permutation $p$-values
control nodewise rejection under the complete permutation null; the same
argument covers a max-type Stage~B threshold test that replaces the
per-threshold Bonferroni rule and cuts the permutation cost of a node from
order $K^2/\alpha$ to order $BK$ for $K$ candidate thresholds; rerun on the
full benchmark it moves the forest by at most two positions. CIF ties for
3rd among 17 classification methods on 21 datasets and ranks 2nd among 18
regression methods on 8 datasets; on the smaller complete-case panels it ranks
6th and 5th, and leave-one-dataset-out configuration selection moves it by
less than half a rank.
With Bonferroni correction held fixed, the runtime ablations locate the cost
in the threshold search: turning off threshold scanning slows forest fits by
8--29$\times$ and exact threshold search by 1.3--20$\times$, while adaptive
stopping is inert at the benchmark's minimum permutation budget (0.9--1.0$\times$)
and cuts a single tree at the larger auto budget to 0.15 of its exhaustive time;
downstream score changes are at most 0.009. Sparse high-$p$
simulations indicate that forest feature sampling can leave informative features
out of many split decisions. Overall,
the results support CIF as a top-$k$ feature-ranking method in the
evaluated downstream prediction benchmarks.
