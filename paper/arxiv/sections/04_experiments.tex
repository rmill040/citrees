\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:experiments}

The experiments compare the rankings produced by CIT and CIF with established
feature rankers and measure what the conditional-inference controls cost. For
each training fold, a method produces a feature ranking, and fixed downstream
learners evaluate the selected features on the held-out fold.
\Cref{sec:results} reports the real-data benchmark with its robustness checks
and CIF ranking ablations, the runtime ablations, and the Stage~B construction
comparison with its extension to the benchmark; \cref{sec:boundary} reports
high-dimensional behavior, recovery on synthetic data with known informative
features, and the mechanism studies.

\subsection{Benchmark setup}
\label{sec:experiments-protocol}

The benchmark uses 5 random seeds, with 5-fold cross-validation within each
seed. Classification fits logistic regression (LR), support vector machine
(SVM), and k-nearest neighbors (KNN) as downstream models and reports balanced
accuracy, defined as mean recall across classes. Regression fits ridge
regression, support vector regression (SVR), and KNN and reports $R^2$. The main
tables use the standard values $k \in \{5,10,25,50,100\}$.
Downstream learner settings are fixed. Logistic regression uses balanced class
weights and a 1,000-iteration limit; SVM uses an RBF kernel with unit
regularization, $\gamma=\texttt{scale}$, and balanced class weights; KNN uses
5 distance-weighted neighbors; ridge uses unit penalty strength; and SVR uses an
RBF kernel with unit regularization, $\epsilon=0.1$, and $\gamma=\texttt{scale}$.

For each seed and fold, feature-standardization parameters are estimated on the
training fold and then applied to the held-out fold. The ranking method uses only
the standardized training fold and produces the ordered feature list described in
\cref{sec:rank-construction}. For each $k$, we take the top-$k$ features from
that ranking, refit standardization on the selected training columns, apply the
same transform to the selected held-out columns, fit the downstream model on the
selected training data, and evaluate it on the held-out fold.

The downstream evaluation also records performance using the full feature set,
$k=p$, for every dataset. For datasets with more than 100 features, the separate
high-$p$ analysis adds fixed values $k \in \{150,200,300,500,750,1{,}000\}$
and fractional values
$\lceil 0.25p\rceil,\lceil 0.5p\rceil,\lceil 0.75p\rceil$, keeping only values
no larger than $p$. The main tables average only the standard $k$ values.

\subsection{Benchmark comparisons}
\label{sec:experiments-analysis-plan}

The benchmark averages scores in three steps. First, for each
$(\text{dataset}, \text{downstream model}, k)$ combination, scores are averaged
over folds and seeds. Second, each dataset score averages those fold-and-seed
means over downstream models and over the standard $k$ values available for the
methods in that comparison. Third, the tables average dataset scores over
datasets.

The real-data classification benchmark contains 23 datasets. Some method runs
on \nolinkurl{gisette}, \nolinkurl{isolet}, and \nolinkurl{orlraws10P} were
excluded (\cref{app:methods} lists them with their reasons), so the all-method
rank summary uses the 21 datasets with results for every method, which omit
\nolinkurl{gisette} and \nolinkurl{isolet}, and each pairwise
comparison uses the datasets where both methods are present. Regression
contains 8 datasets with every method present. Two smaller complete-case
panels, used for bootstrap intervals, omnibus rank tests, and LODO
configuration checks, retain only datasets with every method, every downstream
learner, and all five standard values of $k$: 13 classification datasets and 6
regression datasets. By-$k$ results are reported separately for
$k=5,10,25,50,100$; the corresponding classification dataset counts are
$21, 20, 14, 14, 13$ and the regression counts are $8, 8, 7, 7, 6$, because a
dataset with fewer than $k$ features does not enter the column for $k$.

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:experiments-datasets}

The real-data benchmark includes 23 classification datasets and 8 regression
datasets. The separate high-$p$ analysis restricts attention to the
15 classification datasets and 6 regression datasets with more than 100
features. The full inventories of the real and synthetic datasets are listed in
\Cref{app:dataset-inventory}, which also summarizes the synthetic designs and
their parameter choices.

Feature recovery uses 8 synthetic classification datasets and 8 synthetic
regression datasets with known informative features and controlled noise
structure. The simulation designs of the mechanism studies are described in
\cref{sec:experiments-mechanism}.

\subsection{Compared methods}
\label{sec:experiments-methods}

The benchmark includes three types of feature selectors: permutation-test
filters, embedded tree or ensemble methods, and wrapper baselines.
Permutation-test filters compute univariate dependence statistics and rank
features by their permutation $p$-values: multiple correlation (MC) and
randomized dependence coefficient (RDC) for classification, and Pearson
correlation (PC), distance correlation (DC), and RDC for regression. The RDC
filter keeps the empirical-CDF random feature map from
\citet{LopezPaz2013RDC} but replaces the canonical-correlation solve with the
maximum absolute pairwise correlation across projected columns. It uses 10
random projections (20 sine and cosine columns); the sensitivity of the
rankings to that count is reported in \cref{sec:experiments-real}.
Distance correlation follows
\citet{Szekely2007DistanceCorrelation}.

Embedded methods rank features using tree or ensemble importance scores. These
methods are CART decision trees (DT), single randomized trees with random split
thresholds (RT), CIT, CIF, random
forests (RF) \citep{Breiman2001RandomForests}, ExtraTrees
\citep{Geurts2006ExtraTrees}, XGBoost \citep{ChenGuestrin2016XGBoost},
LightGBM \citep{Ke2017LightGBM}, CatBoost
\citep{Prokhorenkova2018CatBoost}, and the partykit reference implementations
ctree and cforest
\citep{RPartykitPackage,Hothorn2006UnbiasedRecursivePartitioning,HothornZeileis2015Partykit}.
Wrapper baselines score features through auxiliary random-forest fits: Boruta
\citep{Kursa2010Boruta}, permutation importance (PI), which permutes the columns of a 20 percent held-out
split of the training fold
\citep{Breiman2001RandomForests,Strobl2007BiasRFVariableImportance},
conditional permutation importance (CPI)
\citep{DebeerStrobl2020ConditionalPermutationImportanceRevisited}, and
random-forest recursive feature elimination (RF-RFE)
\citep{Guyon2002RFE,Pedregosa2011ScikitLearn}.

In this benchmark, CPI uses stratified conditional permutation with
correlation-based strata. For each target feature, it trains a 100-tree random
forest on a training split and scores a 20 percent validation split. It then
identifies validation-set features with absolute correlation at least 0.5 with
the target feature, forms five-bin percentile strata from those conditioning
features, and permutes the target feature within strata. If no conditioning
feature meets the correlation threshold, the target feature is permuted globally. PI and CPI therefore score a 20 percent split rather than every training row,
which makes them noisier than an out-of-bag estimate; the random forest ranked
by out-of-bag permutation importance (\cref{tab:rf-oobperm-benchmark}) is the
comparator that uses every row.

Ranking rules use each method's natural score direction. Embedded tree and
ensemble baselines rank features by decreasing fitted importance: DT, RT, RF,
ExtraTrees, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. Permutation-test filters rank by
increasing $p$-value, with dependence score as the secondary key. The ctree
reference ranks features by split-use counts. For cforest, we use partykit's
unconditional out-of-bag permutation importance at its default of one permutation per tree, averaged over the 100 trees, which is the only importance the package provides for forests; CIF uses
split-importance rankings from accepted conditional-inference splits. The two conditional-inference forests therefore differ in importance mechanism as well as in how their trees are grown (candidate thresholds, the Stage~B rule, stopping, honesty, and muting), so the CIF-versus-cforest margins below compare the two packages' rankings; the mechanism-matched check of \cref{sec:experiments-mechanism} separates the two. Boruta and recursive feature elimination rank by their fitted rank variables,
with smaller ranks first. PI and CPI rank by decreasing importance; the CPI row
uses the stratified conditional-permutation procedure described above. Ties are
broken by a deterministic order for reproducibility; for the importance-based
rankers, including CIT and CIF, that order is the reversed NumPy sort of
\cref{sec:method}.

\subsection{Configuration selection}
\label{sec:experiments-repro}

Six methods were evaluated under several candidate configurations per task:
four each for CIT and CIF, five for XGBoost, four for cforest, and two each for
LightGBM and ctree. Permutation-test filters used one configuration per
statistic, and methods with one configuration are treated as fixed. For each
task, the paper reports one configuration per method, selected by mean score
over real datasets, downstream models, and the standard values
$k \in \{5,10,25,50,100\}$, and holds it fixed across the datasets of that
task. The four selected conditional-inference configurations
(\cref{tab:selected-configs}) are the MC selector for CIT in classification and
the RDC selector for CIT in regression, and for CIF the RDC selector in both
tasks with 100 bootstrap trees and square-root feature sampling, without honesty
in classification and with honesty at an honesty fraction of 0.5 in regression.
Every CIT and CIF candidate uses the minimum budget, under which adaptive
stopping is inert (\cref{prop:adaptive-size}); the feature correction and the
threshold correction; the per-threshold rule; feature scanning, threshold
scanning, and feature muting; and a 256-bin histogram, which yields up to 257
candidate thresholds per node. The candidates differ in selector and, for CIF,
in honesty.

Because configuration selection and reporting use the same real-data
benchmark, the reported scores, intervals, and rank summaries are descriptive.
As a sensitivity check, we repeat configuration selection in leave-one-dataset-out
(LODO) form for both tasks, on the complete-case panels and on the all-method
panels of the main table. Each dataset in the panel is evaluated using
configurations chosen from the other datasets in that panel (12 of 13 and 20 of
21 in classification, 5 of 6 and 7 of 8 in regression). The downstream models,
$k$ values, and averaging within each dataset are unchanged.

For the synthetic analysis, configurations are selected separately from the
real-data benchmark. Each method is assigned the configuration with the highest
average recovery of the true informative features over $k = 5, 10, 25, 50,$ and
$100$.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:experiments-ablations}

The runtime ablation refits the selected CIT and CIF configurations on the
runtime panel with one control changed at a time: adaptive stopping, feature
scanning, threshold scanning, and feature muting are switched off; the feature
and threshold corrections are removed together; the 256-bin histogram is
replaced by exact thresholds or, for CIF, by a 32-bin histogram; and, for CIF,
bootstrap sampling is switched off and the max-type rule replaces the
per-threshold rule. Both estimators are also refitted at the auto budget, with
adaptive stopping and with no stopping, because the minimum budget leaves the
stopping rule no room to act. Scanning and stopping are independent switches,
so each row measures one mechanism. Each dataset is fitted at five seeds; every
time is the second of two identical fits, so compilation is excluded. Single
trees use the full thread pool, and forests fit 100 trees with one worker per
tree and one Numba thread per worker, all on one 32-core machine.

Two further runs test whether the stopping result depends on how the work is
scheduled or on dataset size. A single-worker forest, with the full thread pool
inside each tree, switches adaptive stopping off on 21 of the 23 runtime
datasets, all except the real waveform and california datasets. On the 14
Benchmark-size datasets, the tree is refitted with adaptive stopping off, with
threshold scanning off, and without the two corrections, and the forest with
adaptive stopping off.

To isolate which parts of the CIF ranking matter, a ranking ablation on the
ablation panel (22 classification and 8 regression datasets) holds the selected
CIF configuration fixed and changes one component at a time: replacing fitted
split importance with split counts, disabling feature muting, disabling
bootstrap sampling, or reducing the forest to one tree. Each variant is
evaluated with the same downstream learners, folds, seeds, and standard top-$k$
values as the main benchmark, and every comparison is paired with the reference
configuration within seed and fold.

\subsection{Stage~B construction comparison}
\label{sec:experiments-stageB}

This study compares the per-threshold rule and the max-type rule of
\cref{eq:maxt-stat} under the MC and PC selectors, with adaptive stopping and
with no stopping; each part was fixed in advance and run at five seeds unless
stated otherwise. Both rules run at the auto budget, which exceeds the
$\lceil 1/\alpha \rceil$ floor, so adaptive stopping can act here, unlike at
the benchmark's minimum budget; the max-type rule uses 999 permutations under
every budget rule. Candidate thresholds come from the histogram method at
settings of 16, 64, and 256 bins, which yields one more candidate than the
setting, capped at the number of distinct values in the node, so the 256-bin
setting yields 100 and 200 candidates at $n=100$ and $n=200$.

\paragraph{Null calibration.}
The first part estimates the false-split rate of a depth-one tree under the
global null, with five independent Gaussian predictors and an independent
response, at $n\in\{100,200,500\}$ and the three bin settings, with 2,000
replicates per cell and seed. The rate measures the complete node decision, the
Stage~A feature test over five predictors followed by the Stage~B threshold
test, and is therefore below the level of the Stage~B test alone.

\paragraph{Stage-B-only study.}
To measure the threshold test by itself, the Stage-B-only study uses one
Gaussian predictor, disables the Stage~A feature test
(\texttt{alpha\_selector} $=1$), and fits a depth-one tree at nominal Stage~B
levels 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.35, and 0.50, at the same $n$ and bin
settings, with 500 null replicates per cell, level, and seed over three seeds.
It repeats the planted step of the power part below with 200 replicates per
cell, level, and seed and reports power at matched realized size: within each
cell, power is interpolated against realized size to a common size of 0.05, or
to the largest realized size both rules reach when the per-threshold rule does
not reach 0.05 within the nominal grid. Two reruns bound the Monte Carlo error
of that comparison: a fourth seed with 1,500 null and 1,000 alternative
replicates per cell and level, and an off-centre design that moves the planted
step from the median to the 85th percentile of the predictor.

\paragraph{Scaling.}
The second part times one tree on ten Gaussian predictors with a planted linear
signal in three of them, at $n\in\{1{,}000,4{,}000,16{,}000\}$ and the same bin
settings, as the median of three fits with a 1,200-second cap; a fit exceeding
the cap is reported as censored. A fixed-budget control arm, timed in the same
run, holds the per-threshold rule at 999 permutations per candidate
(\cref{app:stageB-tables}).

\paragraph{Real-data fits.}
The third part fits one tree on the six Stage~B real datasets of
\cref{tab:panels} at the 256-bin setting with five-fold cross-validation,
recording held-out score, tree depth, fitting time, and the Spearman agreement
of the two rules' feature importances within each fold.

\paragraph{Planted-effect power.}
The fourth part measures the split rate under a planted effect: a depth-one
tree on five Gaussian predictors whose response depends on the first through a
step at zero, with the class-probability shift $\delta\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20\}$ in
classification and the mean shift $\delta\in\{0.2,0.4,0.8\}$ in regression, at
the same sample sizes and bin settings, with 500 replicates per cell and seed.
It records the rate of any split, the rate of a split on the planted predictor,
and the absolute error of the chosen threshold. All parts of this study run on
the same machine as the runtime ablations.

\subsection{Head-to-head forest timing}
\label{sec:experiments-h2h}

The head-to-head timing compares the two Stage~B rules in full forests. It fits
100-tree classification forests in the configuration recommended by
\citet{MilletichDownesHirst2026citrees} (MC selector, minimum budget, adaptive
stopping, the feature and threshold corrections, scanning, muting, and the
256-bin histogram, with depth at most 3), once with the per-threshold rule and
once with the max-type rule, in one run on one machine. The cells are the ten
datasets of the head-to-head panel and a synthetic grid of Gaussian predictors
with a weak or strong planted signal at $n\in\{500, 2{,}000, 8{,}000\}$ with
$p\in\{20,100\}$, at $p=500$ with $n\in\{500, 2{,}000\}$, and at
$n=32{,}000$ with $p\in\{20,100\}$. Each time is the second of two identical
fits, so compilation is excluded.

\subsection{Max-type extension}
\label{sec:experiments-maxt}

The max-type extension asks what the max-type rule does to the benchmark
rankings. It reruns the four CIT and four CIF candidate configurations with the
max-type rule in place of the per-threshold rule and nothing else changed, on
every real and synthetic benchmark dataset (the max-type panel of
\cref{tab:panels}) at the five benchmark seeds. The downstream evaluation,
configuration selection, and ranking follow
\cref{sec:experiments-protocol,sec:experiments-analysis-plan,sec:experiments-repro}
unchanged, with the max-type family standing in for its per-threshold
counterpart.

\subsection{Mechanism studies}
\label{sec:experiments-mechanism}

Five studies examine why the forest rankings behave as they do; their results
are in \cref{sec:results,sec:boundary}.

\paragraph{Mechanism-matched check.}
CIF ranks by split importance and cforest by out-of-bag permutation importance.
The mechanism-matched check (\cref{app:cif-perm-check}) refits CIF on the
mechanism-matched panel and ranks it by cforest's own out-of-bag permutation
importance, so that the comparison with cforest holds the importance mechanism
fixed.

\paragraph{Out-of-bag random forest.}
A further comparator ranks the same 100-tree random forest as the RF ranker by
out-of-bag permutation importance, which, unlike PI and CPI, scores
every training row (\cref{tab:rf-oobperm-benchmark}).

\paragraph{Feature use in sampled forests.}
A study of feature sampling uses $n=250$ and varies $p \in \{100, 500,
1{,}000\}$ and $n_{\mathrm{informative}} \in \{1,2,5,10\}$. It compares CIT,
DT, RT, CIF, CIF-all, RF, and ExtraTrees, using 1,000 trees for the forest
methods. CIF-all denotes the CIF variant that evaluates all nonconstant
features at each split.

\paragraph{Weak-signal cardinality designs.}
These designs test whether high-cardinality noise features displace weak
informative ones. Each has $n=1{,}000$ and 10 informative Gaussian features
among 60 noise features (25 with 500 levels, 25 binary, and 10 Gaussian) in
shuffled order, at signal strengths 0.05, 0.10, and 0.20. The top-ten
composition is scored over 25 fits, and the downstream score at
$k\in\{5,10,25\}$. The rankers are CIF, CIF with the max-type rule, CIF without
the threshold correction, CIT, RF impurity, RF out-of-bag permutation
importance, XGBoost, and LightGBM; CIF and CIT use the MC selector in
classification and the PC selector in regression.

\paragraph{Support measurement.}
The support measurement counts, on each of the 16 synthetic datasets and over
25 fits, the features that win at least one split in CIF, CIT, and RF, and how
many of them are informative.
