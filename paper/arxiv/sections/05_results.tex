\paragraph{CIF ablations.}
\Cref{tab:cif-ranking-ablation} compares the selected CIF configuration with
four targeted changes on the real-data benchmark datasets: split-count ranking,
disabling bootstrap sampling, disabling feature muting, and using one tree.
Each changed variant is paired with the reference in the same seed and fold.
In classification the picture is simple. Reducing CIF to one tree lowers
balanced accuracy on every dataset, by $0.071$ on average, while split-count
ranking, disabling bootstrap sampling, and disabling feature muting change it by
at most $0.003$ on average, with intervals that include zero. Regression is
heterogeneous and the negligible-effect statement does not carry over: the one
tree loses on all eight datasets, by $0.66$ $R^2$ on average and $0.06$ at the
median; split-count ranking and disabling bootstrap sampling lower the mean
$R^2$ by $0.053$ and $0.064$ with intervals that exclude zero, although their
medians are within $0.007$ of zero and the win--loss counts are 2--6 and 4--4;
and disabling feature muting lowers the mean by $0.157$ with a median of zero and
an interval that includes it. The means are pulled down by the three small \nolinkurl{coepra}
datasets, where $R^2$ is unstable across folds, so in regression the controls
matter on a few datasets rather than on average, and the table reports both
statistics.

The complete-case Friedman tests use dataset-level method means.
Classification: $\chi^2(16)=140.46$, $p<0.001$, Kendall's $W=0.68$.
Regression: $\chi^2(17)=26.15$, $p=0.072$, Kendall's $W=0.26$, which does not
reach the 0.05 level, so the regression rank ordering is reported as a
descriptive summary of the 6-dataset panel rather than as evidence of
method differences. The paired bootstrap intervals of CIF against every other method on the same panels (\cref{app:benchmark-stability} and the reference release) are unadjusted post-hoc contrasts, 16 in classification and 17 in regression with no familywise correction, and are read as descriptive. In classification they exclude zero in CIF's favor against CIT, ctree, cforest, the single trees, the permutation filters, PI, and CPI, exclude zero against CIF for RF-RFE, XGBoost, and LightGBM, and include zero for RF, ExtraTrees, CatBoost, and Boruta; in regression, where the omnibus does not reject, no interval excludes zero against CIF and CIF's intervals exclude zero only against the filters, PI, CPI, LightGBM, and ctree. The ranking among the bunched group is therefore not resolved by the data. These intervals and rank summaries describe the
benchmark-selected configurations. The LODO analysis below checks whether those
configurations are stable under one-dataset holdout.

The complete-case panels rank CIF lower than the main table does, and the
LODO reselection accounts for little of the difference. On the 13-dataset
classification panel, the benchmark-selected CIF configuration has mean rank
6.38 (tied 6th of 17); LODO reselection moves it to 6.85 (8th) and reselects the
main-benchmark configuration in 11 of 13 holdouts. On the 6-dataset regression
panel, the fixed configuration has mean rank 7.00 (tied 5th of 18) and LODO
moves it to 7.33 (6th), behind ExtraTrees, CatBoost, RF-RFE, RF, and RT,
reselecting the global configuration in 4 of 6 holdouts. The 3rd and 2nd
places in \cref{tab:method-main-comparison} are therefore properties of the
21- and 8-dataset panels; the smaller complete-case panels drop CIF by three to
four places, and configuration reselection adds less than half a rank. On the 21- and 8-dataset panels themselves, reselection moves CIF from 5.86 (tied 3rd) to 6.10 (5th) in classification and from 6.25 to 6.50 (2nd in both) in regression, reselecting the benchmark configuration in 20 of 21 and 7 of 8 holdouts, with held-out scores lower by 0.002 balanced accuracy and 0.008 $R^2$ (\cref{tab:lodo-config-sensitivity-allmethod}); every single-configuration family keeps its score, and its rank moves only through the reselection of others, by at most 0.05 in classification and 0.125 (one place on eight datasets) in regression.

\subsection{Runtime ablations}
\label{sec:results-practical}

on fitting time and report how much the ranking scores move when those settings
change. Each row switches one control off and reports elapsed wall-clock fit time
relative to the selected configuration for the same estimator, as the median
over datasets of the per-dataset ratio, in each of the four task groups
(classification and regression, real and synthetic). Ratios above 1 are slower
than the reference and ratios below 1 are faster. Scanning and early stopping are
independent switches, so each row measures one mechanism. CIT and CIF are shown
separately because the CIT run records fit time and synthetic top-10 feature
recovery, while the CIF runs also record downstream score changes. The main
benchmark remains the primary performance comparison.

\paragraph{CIT runtime ablations.}
\Cref{tab:cit-runtime-hyperparams} separates two things the earlier coupled
knob conflated. Under the benchmark's \texttt{minimum} budget the adaptive
stopping rule is inert by construction (\cref{prop:adaptive-size}): the budget
equals the floor below which the rule cannot stop, so switching it off changes
fitting time by 0.87--0.98$\times$ and leaves every tree, and therefore every
recovery score, unchanged. Where the rule can act it earns its place: at the
\texttt{auto} budget the adaptive tree costs 1.15--1.54$\times$ the default
while the same budget without stopping costs 3.5--7.9$\times$, so within each
task group adaptive stopping brings the larger budget's tree to 0.15--0.41 of
its exhaustive time at a recovery change of at most 0.016. The control that matters for a single tree is the
threshold scan: testing candidate thresholds in order of promise and stopping at
the first rejection makes fits 2.7--32$\times$ faster than testing every
candidate, with the largest ratio on the real regression datasets where
predictors admit the full 256 candidates. Feature scanning matters less for a
single tree (0.57--2.6$\times$, faster in one group because the prescan itself
has a cost), and exact threshold search and feature muting are within a few
percent of the default. Removing the Bonferroni correction changes the
statistical rule: trees grow from a mean depth of 4.8 and 6.9 to 9.9 and 14.4
levels and top-10 recovery moves by up to 0.063, so it is not a runtime
control. The pattern holds on the 14 benchmark-size datasets (up to 20,000
observations and 5,000 features; five seeds each): switching adaptive stopping
off changes single-tree time by 0.58--1.04$\times$ (medians 0.95 in
classification and 0.80 in regression), threshold scanning saves a median
18$\times$ in classification and up to 44$\times$ on spam, and dropping the
Bonferroni correction runs in 0.01--1.08$\times$ of the default. The largest
single-tree time is gisette at 31 minutes, where each of 5,000 features
receives a Bonferroni-scaled budget.

\paragraph{CIF runtime ablations.}
\Cref{tab:cif-runtime-hyperparams} tells the same story for forests. Adaptive
stopping is inert at the benchmark budget (0.96--0.99$\times$, no score
change), and a single-worker forest confirms that the result does not depend on
the trees hiding behind the thread pool: with one worker and the full thread
pool per tree, switching the rule off changes fitting time by 0.79--1.10$\times$
across 21 datasets. Where the rule can act, at the \texttt{auto} budget, the
adaptive forest costs 1.27--1.83$\times$ the default and the same budget without
stopping 5.2--14$\times$, so the adaptive forest runs in 0.11--0.26 of its
exhaustive time within each task group, with synthetic recovery changes of at
most 0.030. The threshold scan is again the dominant control, at
7.9--29$\times$ by group medians, and the number of candidates is the next:
exact threshold search is 1.3--20$\times$ slower than the 256-bin histogram
and a 32-bin histogram is 7--20$\times$ faster, with downstream score changes
of at most 0.009 and recovery changes of at most 0.018. Feature
scanning costs 0.99--5.1$\times$, with its largest effect on the real regression
datasets. The max-type Stage~B test fits in 0.14--0.55 of the default time with
downstream changes of at most 0.005; \cref{sec:results-stageB} reports its
calibration, power, and ranking effect. Removing the Bonferroni correction is
the fastest change for both CIT and CIF, but it changes the statistical rule and
yields deeper trees with more selected features; the selected setting keeps
Bonferroni correction. On the 14 benchmark-size datasets the forest's
adaptive-stopping ratio is 0.77--1.09$\times$ (medians 1.00 and 0.99).

\subsection{Stage~B construction comparison}
\label{sec:results-stageB}

The complete-node false-split rate is below 0.05 for both Stage~B constructions in all 36 design cells, 72 measurements pooled over five seeds (\cref{tab:stageB-calibration}); the constructions differ in how far below. Under the per-threshold Bonferroni rule the rate falls as the bin setting grows, from 0.0196 at 16 bins to 0.0070 at 256 in classification with $n=500$ and exhaustive stopping, because adjacent thresholds induce nearly identical splits and the union bound charges for each. Under the max-type rule it stays between 0.028 and 0.038 in every cell and rises slightly with the bin setting, with cell means of 0.032, 0.034, and 0.034. These complete-node rates are dominated by the Stage~A gate, which must first reject over five independent predictors, so they bound the Stage~B test from below rather than measure it. The Stage-B-only companion (\cref{tab:stageB-only}, \cref{app:stageB-tables}), which fixes the tested predictor without the response and disables the gate, measures the test itself: at nominal level 0.05 the per-threshold Bonferroni rule has size 0.000--0.027 with means of 0.018, 0.011, and 0.006 at 16, 64, and 256 bins, and the max-type rule 0.023--0.044 with means of 0.033, 0.035, and 0.035; every 95\% Clopper--Pearson upper limit is below 0.056, and the cells whose intervals include 0.05 are max-type cells at 64 and 256 bins. Both constructions are therefore valid at the node, and the reverse cardinality bias of \cref{sec:method} is the Bonferroni size falling by a factor of three across the bin settings while the max-type size does not move.

The cost difference is where the constructions separate
(\cref{tab:stageB-scaling}, \cref{app:stageB-tables}). A control arm run in the same timing pass (\cref{tab:stageB-fixed-budget}) holds the per-threshold rule at a fixed budget of 999 permutations per candidate: at 16 bins it matches the scaled rule in size, power, and time; at 64 and 256 bins it costs 3.7 seconds at $n=16{,}000$ against 90 for the scaled rule and 6.5 for the max-type rule, but it never splits (size 0, power 0, depth 0 in every cell), because $\alpha/K$ falls below the resolution $1/(B+1)$. The $K^2/\alpha$ budget is therefore what the per-threshold rule needs in order to reject, not a choice, and the max-type rule is the construction that resolves the level at $999K$. With exhaustive stopping, one tree at the 256-bin
setting takes 5.7 seconds at $n=1{,}000$, 14 seconds at $n=4{,}000$, and 60 seconds at $n=16{,}000$ under the Bonferroni rule in classification (1.1, 8.3, and 51 seconds in regression); the max-type rule takes 0.5, 1.6, and 6.5 seconds at the same settings. The ratio grows with the bin setting as the $K^2/\alpha$ against $BK$ accounting predicts: the two rules cost the same at 16 bins, the exhaustive ratio is 1.2--1.9 at 64 and 2.0--11 at 256. Adaptive stopping shortens the Bonferroni test once its budget exceeds the $\lceil 1/\alpha \rceil$ floor, by 1.3--3.0$\times$ at 64 bins and 2.3--6.9$\times$ at 256, so with adaptive stopping the max-type advantage is 1.0--1.8 at 64 bins and 1.3--6.4 at 256. Depth barely separates the rules on these data: over the grid the max-type trees have 5.0--11.0 levels and the Bonferroni trees 4.0--10.0 under either stopping mode. The planted signal is linear in three of the ten predictors, so depth reflects both power at small nodes and splits on noise; the real-data scores are the measure of which trees predict better.

On the six real datasets (\cref{tab:stageB-real}, \cref{app:stageB-tables}), with scores taken as the median over five seeds of the fold assignment, the max-type rule matched or exceeded the Bonferroni rule in 8 of the 12 dataset and stopping-mode cells: by 0.022 and 0.026 on vowel, 0.069 and 0.026 on facebook, 0.061 and 0.033 on imports-85, and 0.008 and 0.004 on residential; on page-blocks and spam it was lower by at most 0.004. Its fitting time was 0.9 to 2.3 times shorter with adaptive stopping and 0.9 to 4.8 times shorter with exhaustive stopping; the savings are largest on page-blocks and facebook, whose predictors admit the most candidates per node, and the max-type rule is slower only on residential, by about 10 percent, and equal on imports-85. The Spearman agreement of the two rules' feature importances ranges from 0.30 to 0.91, so the max-type rule produces a different ranking rather than a faster route to the same one. In the head-to-head forest timing on the recommended configuration, with both rules refit in one run on the same hosts, the max-type rule is 2.9--12$\times$ faster than the per-threshold rule on synthetic Gaussian predictors at $n\in\{500, 2{,}000, 8{,}000\}$ and $p\in\{20,100,500\}$ and 20--32$\times$ faster at $n=32{,}000$; on the ten real datasets it reduces madelon from 24 to 4.8 seconds, gamma from 45 to 22 seconds, and isolet from 19 to 11 seconds, and changes the other seven by at most 2 seconds.

The power study (\cref{tab:stageB-power}, \cref{app:stageB-tables}) plants a step in one of five Gaussian predictors and records how often a depth-one tree splits at all and how often on the planted predictor. At a common nominal level of 0.05 the max-type rule splits more often than the Bonferroni rule in every cell short of saturation, by 0.03 on average and up to 0.12 in the complete-node design and by 0.10 on average in the Stage-B-only design, with the gap growing with the candidate count: 0.015 at 16 bins, 0.029 at 64, and 0.049 at 256 in the complete-node design, because the max-type power is flat in the bin setting while the Bonferroni power falls as the adjustment tightens. That nominal-level gap is a size gap, not a power gap. Matching the two rules at the same realized size in the Stage-B-only design (\cref{tab:stageB-only}) removes it: the max-type minus Bonferroni difference in size-matched power ranges from $-0.054$ to $+0.034$ over the 108 cell and effect combinations, with mean $-0.006$ and no trend in the bin setting ($-0.005$, $-0.008$, $-0.006$ at 16, 64, and 256 bins). In four of the nine classification cells (64 bins at $n=100$ and 256 bins at every $n$) and one regression cell ($n=500$, 256 bins) the Bonferroni rule does not reach realized size 0.05 within the nominal grid, its size at nominal 0.50 being 0.031--0.046, so the match in those cells is at that largest realized size; the other 13 cells match at 0.05. Adaptive and exhaustive stopping agree within 0.014 in every cell. Two further runs bound the Monte Carlo error of that comparison (\texttt{paper\_stageb\_power\_replicates.csv}): a fourth seed with 1,500 null and 1,000 alternative replicates per cell and level, and a run with the planted step moved from the median to the 85th percentile of the predictor, the tail case the standardization of \cref{sec:method} is designed for. With the larger replicate counts the size-matched difference is $-0.006$ on average across the 96 cell and effect combinations completed (range $-0.040$ to $+0.018$; the exhaustive 256-bin cells at $n=500$, the $K^2/\alpha$ worst case, were not completed at this replicate count); a parametric bootstrap of the whole matching procedure, resampling every null and alternative count and redoing the interpolation, puts a standard error of about 0.017 on each difference, so the smallest difference the design could detect is about 0.03, and one of the 96 exceeds two standard errors; with the off-centre step the difference is $-0.004$ on average (range $-0.051$ to $+0.039$, bootstrap standard error about 0.038, detectable difference about 0.08) and none of the 108 exceeds two standard errors, while the Stage-B-only sizes at nominal 0.05 stay at 0.000--0.026 for the Bonferroni rule and 0.018--0.046 for the max-type rule. The cost of the joint test is precision: the max-type threshold sits a median 0.19 standard deviations from the planted cut against 0.15 for the Bonferroni rule, and 87 rather than 90 percent of its detections fall on the planted predictor. The max-type rule is therefore calibrated closer to the nominal level, cheaper on continuous predictors, and shows no detectable difference in power at matched size within the Monte Carlo error of these designs, with a small loss in the location of the chosen threshold; what it gains at a fixed nominal level is the size that the per-threshold adjustment gives up.

Rerunning the four CIT and four CIF configurations under the max-type rule on the full ranking benchmark answers what the rule does to the rankings (\cref{tab:maxt-benchmark-rankings}). Of the 1,792 Stage~A cells (configuration, dataset, seed triples over the eight configurations and the benchmark's real and synthetic datasets), 1,785 completed; the seven that did not are CIF with the RDC selector and honesty off on isolet, gisette, and letter, which terminate the compute host in the same way as the RDC exclusions of the per-threshold benchmark (\cref{app:methods}); their sibling seeds and honesty-on twins completed, so on letter the max-type CIF score averages the three seeds that completed. Selecting one configuration per family and ranking on the all-method panels of 21 and 8 datasets exactly as in \cref{sec:experiments-real}, the max-type forest moves from position 3.5 to 5 of 17 in classification (mean rank 5.86 to 6.31) and from 2 to 4 of 18 in regression (6.25 to 7.50), and the max-type tree keeps its position in both tasks (16 of 17 and 14 of 18). Paired on the cells both rules completed, the per-threshold rule scores higher by 0.0014 (CIF) and 0.0022 (CIT) balanced accuracy in classification and by 0.0001 (CIF) and 0.0085 (CIT) $R^2$ in regression, and is the higher of the two on 59 to 63 percent of datasets. The max-type rule is therefore a cost and calibration choice, not a ranking gain: it changes which features the trees rank, as the importance agreement above shows, without changing how the rankings perform downstream.
