\paragraph{CIF ablations.}
\Cref{tab:cif-ranking-ablation} compares the selected CIF configuration with
four targeted changes on the real-data benchmark datasets: split-count ranking,
disabling bootstrap sampling, disabling feature muting, and using one tree.
Each changed variant is paired with the reference in the same seed and fold.
Reducing CIF to one tree is the only change with a material effect: it lowers
the score on every dataset in both tasks, by $0.071$ balanced accuracy on
average in classification. Split-count ranking, disabling bootstrap sampling,
and disabling feature muting change classification balanced accuracy by at most
$0.001$ on average, with intervals that include zero. The regression means for
these three changes are pulled down by the three small \nolinkurl{coepra}
datasets, where $R^2$ is unstable across folds; their medians are within
$0.007$ of zero and their win--loss counts are near even.

The complete-case Friedman tests use dataset-level method means.
Classification: $\chi^2(16)=140.46$, $p<0.001$, Kendall's $W=0.68$.
Regression: $\chi^2(17)=26.15$, $p=0.072$, Kendall's $W=0.26$, which does not
reach the 0.05 level, so the regression rank ordering is reported as a
descriptive summary of the 6-dataset panel rather than as evidence of
method differences. These intervals and rank summaries describe the
benchmark-selected configurations. The LODO analysis below checks whether those
configurations are stable under one-dataset holdout.

The complete-case panels rank CIF lower than the main table does, and the
LODO reselection accounts for little of the difference. On the 13-dataset
classification panel, the benchmark-selected CIF configuration has mean rank
6.38 (tied 6th of 17); LODO reselection moves it to 6.85 (8th) and reselects the
main-benchmark configuration in 11 of 13 holdouts. On the 6-dataset regression
panel, the fixed configuration has mean rank 7.00 (tied 5th of 18) and LODO
moves it to 7.33 (6th), behind ExtraTrees, CatBoost, RF-RFE, RF, and RT,
reselecting the global configuration in 4 of 6 holdouts. The 3rd and 2nd
places in \cref{tab:method-main-comparison} are therefore properties of the
21- and 8-dataset panels; the smaller complete-case panels drop CIF by three to
four places, and configuration reselection adds less than half a rank.
\Cref{tab:cif-ci-comparisons,tab:lodo-config-sensitivity}
give the confidence intervals and LODO details.

\subsection{Runtime ablations}
\label{sec:results-practical}

The runtime ablations identify which benchmark settings have the largest effect
on fitting time and report how much the ranking scores move when those settings
change. Each row changes one setting and reports elapsed wall-clock fit time
relative to the selected configuration for the same estimator. Ratios above 1 are
slower than that reference, and ratios below 1 are faster. CIT and CIF are shown
separately because the CIT
run records fit time and synthetic top-10 feature recovery, while the CIF runs
also record downstream score changes. The main benchmark remains the primary
performance comparison.
\Cref{app:complexity-details} links each ablation to the affected work terms.

\paragraph{CIT runtime ablations.}
\Cref{tab:cit-runtime-hyperparams} shows that adaptive stopping is the setting
that matters for a single tree. Disabling it makes fits 3.2--8.3$\times$
slower by median dataset-level ratios in all four task groups. It also changes
the fitted tree: without early stopping the mean depth on the synthetic
datasets rises from 5.8 to 9.0 (4.8 to 7.7 in classification, 7.1 to 10.6 in
regression), and top-10 feature recovery moves
by up to 0.40, with the largest changes on the Toeplitz and confounder designs
and none on the weak-signal designs. A single tree exposes every difference in
the realized sequence of features, thresholds, and nodes, which the forest
averages away. Exact threshold search and feature scanning change runtime by
less than a factor of 3.2 in either direction and recovery by at most 0.06 and
0.38.

\paragraph{CIF runtime ablations.}
\Cref{tab:cif-runtime-hyperparams} shows that adaptive stopping is also the
dominant control for forests, and that its value depends on the data. On the
synthetic datasets, disabling it makes fits 6.1--6.5$\times$ slower by median
dataset-level ratios. On the real datasets the same change makes fits
483--717$\times$ slower: with hundreds of predictors and a Bonferroni-adjusted
level, a null predictor needs thousands of permutations to be rejected without
early stopping, and adaptive stopping dismisses it after a few dozen. Exact
threshold search is 1.1--21$\times$ slower than histogram-256 by group mean
elapsed times, with the largest ratio on the real regression datasets, and
disabling feature scanning costs 1.2--4.2$\times$. Downstream score changes
are at most 0.010 and synthetic feature-recovery changes at most 0.010 across
all three rows. Removing Bonferroni correction is faster for both CIT and CIF,
but it changes the statistical rule and yields trees with more selected
features; the selected setting keeps Bonferroni correction.

\subsection{Stage~B construction comparison}
\label{sec:results-stageB}

The complete-node false-split rate is below 0.05 for both Stage~B constructions in all 36 design cells, 72 measurements (\cref{tab:stageB-calibration}); the constructions differ in how far below. Under the per-threshold Bonferroni rule the rate falls as the bin setting grows, from 0.0145 at 16 bins to 0.0040 at 256 in classification with $n=500$ and exhaustive stopping, because adjacent thresholds induce nearly identical splits and the union bound charges for each. Under the max-type rule it stays between 0.025 and 0.038 in every cell and rises slightly with the bin setting, with cell means of 0.030, 0.032, and 0.032.

The cost difference is where the constructions separate
(\cref{tab:stageB-scaling}, \cref{app:stageB-tables}). With exhaustive stopping, one tree at the 256-bin
setting takes 60 seconds at $n=1{,}000$ and 426 to 486 seconds at
$n=4{,}000$ under the Bonferroni rule and exceeds the 900-second cap at
$n=16{,}000$; the max-type rule takes 0.5, 1.5 to 1.6, and 6.2 to 6.7 seconds
at the same settings. The exhaustive ratio grows with the bin setting, from 2.0--6.4 at 16 bins to 14--49 at 64 and 119--318 at 256, faster than the linear growth in $K$ that the $K^2/\alpha$ against $BK$ accounting alone predicts. With adaptive stopping the Bonferroni rule dismisses most candidates after a few dozen permutations and the ratio is 1.6--4.7 at 16 bins, 3.2--16 at 64, and 5.9--84 at 256. The rules also
grow different trees on these data: with adaptive stopping the max-type trees
are deeper (5.3--11.0 levels against 4.0--10.0), and with exhaustive stopping
the Bonferroni trees are deeper (6.7--18.3 against 5.0--10.3). The planted signal is linear in three of the ten predictors, so depth reflects both power at small nodes and splits on noise; the real-data scores are the measure of which trees predict better.

On the six real datasets (\cref{tab:stageB-real}, \cref{app:stageB-tables}) the max-type rule gave
held-out scores at least as high as the Bonferroni rule in 10 of the 12
dataset and stopping-mode cells, by 0.002 to 0.10 with exhaustive stopping and
by 0.005 to 0.031 with adaptive stopping; the exceptions are page-blocks with adaptive stopping, lower by 0.002, and facebook with exhaustive stopping, where the response has a median of 123.5 and a maximum of 6,334, so a single extreme held-out observation can decide a fold's $R^2$ under either rule. Its fitting time was 0.9 to 30 times shorter with adaptive stopping and 1.5 to 155 times shorter with exhaustive stopping. The Spearman agreement of the two rules' feature importances ranges from 0.40 to 0.95, so the max-type rule produces a different ranking rather than a faster route to the same one. In the head-to-head forest timing on the recommended configuration, with both rules refit in one run, the max-type rule is 4.5--15$\times$ faster than the per-threshold rule on synthetic Gaussian predictors at $n\in\{500, 2{,}000, 8{,}000\}$ and $p\in\{20,100,500\}$; on the eight real datasets it reduces madelon from 21 to 4.8 seconds, gamma from 42 to 23 seconds, and musk from 12 to 9.2 seconds, and changes the other five by less than one second.
