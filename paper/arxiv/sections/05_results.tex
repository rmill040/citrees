\paragraph{CIF ablations.}
The CIF ranking ablation compares the selected CIF configuration with four
targeted changes on the eight classification datasets with paired replicates:
split-count ranking, disabling bootstrap sampling, disabling feature muting, and
using one tree (\nolinkurl{ALLAML}, \nolinkurl{CLL\_SUB\_111},
\nolinkurl{ORL}, \nolinkurl{TOX\_171}, \nolinkurl{Yale}, \nolinkurl{arcene},
\nolinkurl{dexter}, and \nolinkurl{gamma}). Each changed variant is paired with
the reference in the same seed and fold, scores are averaged over downstream
learners and supported standard values of $k$, and the intervals are percentile
bootstrap intervals over dataset-level differences from 20{,}000 replicates.
Reducing CIF to one tree has the only material effect: $-0.115$ balanced
accuracy, 95\% CI $[-0.170, -0.067]$, lower on all eight datasets. Split-count
ranking ($+0.000$, $[-0.004, +0.005]$), disabling bootstrap sampling ($+0.003$,
$[-0.002, +0.009]$), and disabling feature muting ($+0.000$, $[-0.003, +0.003]$)
each change the mean score by less than $0.005$ with intervals that include
zero.

The complete-case Friedman tests use dataset-level method means.
Classification: $\chi^2(16)=140.46$, $p<0.001$, Kendall's $W=0.68$.
Regression: $\chi^2(17)=26.15$, $p=0.072$, Kendall's $W=0.26$, which does not
reach the 0.05 level, so the regression rank ordering is reported as a
descriptive summary of the 6-dataset panel rather than as evidence of
method differences. These intervals and rank summaries describe the
benchmark-selected configurations. The LODO analysis below checks whether those
configurations are stable under one-dataset holdout.

In the 13-dataset classification panel, LODO ranks CIF eighth of 17 on average
(mean rank 6.85), and CIF reselects the main-benchmark configuration in 11 of
13 holdouts, using 2 distinct configurations. In the 6-dataset regression
panel, LODO ranks CIF sixth of 18 on average (mean rank 7.33), behind
ExtraTrees, CatBoost, RF-RFE, RF, and RT; CIF reselects the global regression
configuration in 4 of 6 holdouts.
\Cref{tab:cif-ci-comparisons,tab:lodo-config-sensitivity}
give the confidence intervals and LODO details.

\subsection{Runtime ablations}
\label{sec:results-practical}

The runtime ablations identify which benchmark settings have the largest effect
on fitting time and report how much the ranking scores move when those settings
change. Each row changes one setting and reports elapsed wall-clock fit time
relative to the selected configuration for the same estimator. Ratios above 1 are
slower than that reference, and ratios below 1 are faster. CIT and CIF are shown
separately because the CIT
run records fit time and synthetic top-10 feature recovery, while the CIF runs
also record downstream score changes. The main benchmark remains the primary
performance comparison.
\Cref{app:complexity-details} links each ablation to the affected work terms.

\paragraph{CIT runtime ablations.}
\Cref{tab:cit-runtime-hyperparams} shows that adaptive stopping is the setting
that matters for a single tree. Disabling it makes fits 3.2--8.3$\times$
slower by median dataset-level ratios in all four task groups. It also changes
the fitted tree: without early stopping the mean depth on the synthetic
datasets rises from 5.8 to 9.0 (4.8 to 7.7 in classification, 7.1 to 10.6 in
regression), and top-10 feature recovery moves
by up to 0.40, with the largest changes on the Toeplitz and confounder designs
and none on the weak-signal designs. A single tree exposes every difference in
the realized sequence of features, thresholds, and nodes, which the forest
averages away. Exact threshold search and feature scanning change runtime by
less than a factor of 3.2 in either direction and recovery by at most 0.06 and
0.38.

\paragraph{CIF runtime ablations.}
\Cref{tab:cif-runtime-hyperparams} shows that adaptive stopping is also the
dominant control for forests, and that its value depends on the data. On the
synthetic datasets, disabling it makes fits 6.1--6.5$\times$ slower by median
dataset-level ratios. On the real datasets the same change makes fits
483--717$\times$ slower: with hundreds of predictors and a Bonferroni-adjusted
level, a null predictor needs thousands of permutations to be rejected without
early stopping, and adaptive stopping dismisses it after a few dozen. Exact
threshold search is 1.1--21$\times$ slower than histogram-256 by group mean
elapsed times, with the largest ratio on the real regression datasets, and
disabling feature scanning costs 1.2--4.2$\times$. Downstream score changes
are at most 0.010 and synthetic feature-recovery changes at most 0.010 across
all three rows. Removing Bonferroni correction is faster for both CIT and CIF,
but it changes the statistical rule and yields trees with more selected
features; the selected setting keeps Bonferroni correction.

\section{High-dimensional and synthetic analyses}
\label{sec:boundary}

\paragraph{High-dimensional real datasets.}
These analyses study where CIF's scores peak as $k$ grows, how feature
recovery on synthetic datasets changes across values of $k$, and how feature
sampling inside the forest affects how often splits use informative
features.
Across the 15 classification datasets with more than 100 features, CIF first
reaches its best score at an intermediate value of $k$ ($100 < k < p$) in 29 of 45
dataset-learner combinations. It first reaches its best score at the full
feature set ($k=p$) in only 7 of 45 combinations.
\Cref{fig:high-p-boundary} shows this pattern by downstream learner; regression
is summarized next. Classification scores most often peak at
intermediate $k$: 8 of 15 LR datasets, 12 of 15 SVM datasets, and 9 of 15 KNN
datasets. The full feature set is mainly an LR exception. Moving from $k{=}100$
to $k{=}p$ gives a mean $k{=}p$ minus $k{=}100$ score change of $+0.026$ for LR,
and $k=p$ matches the best observed LR score in 7 of 15 datasets. For SVM and
KNN, the corresponding mean changes are $-0.053$ and $-0.071$, and $k=p$ is
best in only 1 of 15 datasets for each learner.

In the 6 high-$p$ regression datasets, full-feature evaluation is rarely best.
Across the 18 dataset-learner combinations, 12 peak at or below $k{=}100$, only
2 peak at $k=p$, and the mean $k{=}p$ minus $k{=}100$ score change is $-0.270$.
The main full-feature exception remains the classification LR result in
\Cref{fig:high-p-boundary}.

\paragraph{Synthetic feature recovery.}
Across synthetic classification datasets, 35.3\% of the features in CIF's top-$k$
list are truly informative on average over the evaluated $k$ values, ranked
8th of the 15 methods with synthetic classification runs, and its top-1 hit
rate is 65.5\%, ranked 10th of 15. Across synthetic
regression datasets, the corresponding values are 39.1\%, ranked 11th of the
16 methods with synthetic regression runs, and
84.5\%, ranked 10th of 16. In both tasks, CIF recovers true features within the
top-$k$ list more reliably than it places one first.
\Cref{tab:synthetic-head} summarizes this contrast, and
\Cref{fig:synthetic-topk-curves} shows recovery as $k$ grows. The percentage of
selected features that are informative falls as $k$ grows. On
redundant-feature synthetic designs, most selected features correspond to true
signals or redundant proxies: 82.4\% for classification and 83.8\% for
regression.

\paragraph{Feature sampling in forests.}
A sparse simulation asks whether forest feature sampling makes splits less
likely to use informative features in classification and regression settings.
\Cref{fig:candidate-forest-counts} shows one sparse classification
design comparing CIF with CIF-all. CIF samples a subset of features at each
split; CIF-all considers every nonconstant feature in Stage~A.
\Cref{app:candidate-availability} gives the classification and regression
results over $p \in \{100,500,1{,}000\}$.

In the single-tree simulations, CIT uses informative features in 100.0\% of
classification splits and 93.5\% of regression splits. In this fixed design,
informative features are usually selected when every nonconstant feature is
considered. In forests, sampling a subset of features reduces how often informative
features are used for splits. Across the classification forest
simulations with 1,000 trees, CIF uses informative features in 33.2\% of
splits, compared with 91.7\% for CIF-all, 8.4\% for RF, and 6.5\% for ET.
CIF also uses more distinct noise features than CIF-all, 265 versus 93
on average. For the $p{=}1{,}000$ classification setting with two informative
features, CIF uses informative features in 9.0\% of splits, while CIF-all uses
them in 100.0\%. In regression, CIF-all uses informative features more often
than CIF, 42.3\% versus 27.8\%, but uses more distinct noise features, 528.8
versus 367.7.
CIF-all isolates feature subsampling because feature muting and
the other tree settings are held fixed. In these sparse high-$p$ simulations,
splits use informative features most often when every nonconstant feature can
enter Stage~A. When the sampled feature subset rarely includes an informative
feature at a node, Stage~A selects from that subset.
