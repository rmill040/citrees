\section{High-dimensional and synthetic analyses}
\label{sec:boundary}

\paragraph{High-dimensional real datasets.}
These analyses study where CIF's scores peak as $k$ grows, how feature
recovery on synthetic datasets changes across values of $k$, and how feature
sampling inside the forest affects how often splits use informative
features.
Across the 15 classification datasets with more than 100 features, CIF first
reaches its best score at an intermediate value of $k$ ($100 < k < p$) in 29 of 45
dataset-learner combinations. It first reaches its best score at the full
feature set ($k=p$) in only 7 of 45 combinations.
\Cref{fig:high-p-boundary} shows this pattern by downstream learner; regression
is summarized next. Classification scores most often peak at
intermediate $k$: 8 of 15 LR datasets, 12 of 15 SVM datasets, and 9 of 15 KNN
datasets. The full feature set is mainly an LR exception. Moving from $k{=}100$
to $k{=}p$ gives a mean $k{=}p$ minus $k{=}100$ score change of $+0.026$ for LR,
and LR first reaches its best score at $k=p$ in 5 of 15 datasets. For SVM and
KNN, the corresponding mean changes are $-0.053$ and $-0.071$, and $k=p$ is
first-best in only 1 of 15 datasets for each learner; the three counts sum to
the 7 of 45 above.

In the 6 high-$p$ regression datasets, full-feature evaluation is rarely best.
Across the 18 dataset-learner combinations, 12 peak at or below $k{=}100$, only
2 peak at $k=p$, and the mean $k{=}p$ minus $k{=}100$ score change is $-0.270$.
The main full-feature exception remains the classification LR result in
\Cref{fig:high-p-boundary}.

\paragraph{Synthetic feature recovery.}
Across synthetic classification datasets, 35.3\% of the features in CIF's top-$k$
list are truly informative on average over the evaluated $k$ values, ranked
8th of the 15 methods with synthetic classification runs, and its top-1 hit
rate is 65.5\%, ranked 10th of 15. Across synthetic
regression datasets, the corresponding values are 39.1\%, ranked 11th of the
16 methods with synthetic regression runs, and
84.5\%, ranked 10th of 16. On both measures and in both tasks CIF sits in the
lower half of the methods; the two measures are not comparable to each other,
since one is a share of the list and the other a hit rate.
\Cref{tab:synthetic-head} summarizes both measures, and
\Cref{fig:synthetic-topk-curves} shows recovery as $k$ grows. The percentage of
selected features that are informative falls as $k$ grows. On
redundant-feature synthetic designs, most selected features correspond to true
signals or redundant proxies: 82.4\% for classification and 83.8\% for
regression.

\paragraph{Feature sampling in forests.}
A sparse simulation asks whether forest feature sampling makes splits less
likely to use informative features in classification and regression settings.
\Cref{fig:candidate-forest-counts} shows one sparse classification
design comparing CIF with CIF-all. CIF samples a subset of features at each
split; CIF-all considers every nonconstant feature in Stage~A.
\Cref{app:candidate-availability} gives the classification and regression
results over $p \in \{100,500,1{,}000\}$.

In the single-tree simulations, CIT uses informative features in 100.0\% of
classification splits and 93.5\% of regression splits. In this fixed design,
informative features are usually selected when every nonconstant feature is
considered. In forests, sampling a subset of features reduces how often informative
features are used for splits. Across the classification forest
simulations with 1,000 trees, CIF uses informative features in 33.2\% of
splits, compared with 91.7\% for CIF-all, 8.4\% for RF, and 6.5\% for ET. This diagnostic counts split usage only, and two qualifications bound it. The gap between CIF and RF or ET is confounded by tree size: RF and ET grow to purity, so most of their splits are deep splits on noise, while CIF stops at the permutation gate. And the CIF-all comparison is the combinatorics of feature sampling rather than a property of conditional inference: with 32 of 1,000 features sampled at a node and two informative, the chance that an informative feature is even a candidate is about 6 percent, which is where CIF sits in that design (9.0 percent of splits) and where random forests sit on average over the designs (8.4 percent). Evaluating every feature at every node removes that floor at a cost no practitioner would pay at this dimension, so its consequence for the ranking was not measured; the section establishes the mechanism, not a remedy.

Two further designs measure the cardinality effects directly in the ranking
(\cref{tab:weak-signal-cardinality}; every ranker and cell is in the reference release). Ten standard normal features carry a common linear signal whose marginal correlation with the response is set to 0.05, 0.10, or 0.20, and they sit among 25 noise columns with 500 levels, 25 binary noise columns, and 10 Gaussian noise columns, with the column order shuffled; each ranker is scored by the composition of its top ten over 25 fits. The two biases the paper describes both appear. At the weakest signal random forest impurity importance fills 38 percent of its top ten with the 500-level noise columns and none with binary noise; the per-threshold CIF fills 31 to 40 percent with binary noise and 24 to 26 percent with the 500-level columns, the reverse cardinality penalty of the per-threshold rule, and the max-type rule reduces the binary share to 26 to 30 percent. Neither bias buys recovery: random forest impurity importance places the most informative features in its top ten at every signal strength (0.42 to 0.43 at 0.05, 0.72 to 0.73 at 0.10, 0.99 at 0.20) against 0.26 to 0.34 for the CIF variants at the weakest signal and 0.64 to 0.66 at 0.10, with standard errors of 0.01 to 0.03, and the downstream scores of all rankers are within 0.02 of each other at every signal. Random forest permutation importance does not remove the preference on these designs: its 500-level share is 0.47 and 0.36 at the two weaker signals, above impurity importance, and its recovery is the lowest of the four rankers. On these designs, then, conditional inference trades one cardinality bias for a different one of comparable size in the top-ten composition, smaller in classification and larger in regression, while keeping a 0.22 to 0.26 share of 500-level noise, and it recovers fewer weak features than impurity importance; the gain in an unbiased ranking shows on predictors whose cardinality carries no signal, as in the clinical application of the companion article, not in recovery of weak marginal effects.

A second measurement, on the synthetic benchmark datasets, records each ranker's support, the features that win at least one split. Random forests give every feature nonzero importance, so their support is the full feature set. On 13 of the 16 designs CIF's support keeps 90 to 100 percent of the planted features while excluding 1 to 93 percent of all features (under 5 percent on the two redundant designs, where the correlated copies all win splits; 41 of 100 features with 9.0 of 10 planted in the standard regression design); on the weak-signal classification, heteroscedastic regression, and 1,000-feature classification designs it keeps 41 to 47 percent of the planted features (4.1 of 10 inside a support of 35 of 100, 4.7 of 10 inside 14 of 50, and 2.1 of 5 inside 91 of 1,000), so the support is a shortlist rather than an estimate of the number of informative features. Beyond the support a ranking is filler in the tie order of \cref{sec:method}, and for a single tree the filler is most of any top-$k$ list: CIT supports hold 0 to 10 features on average, so filler is 52, 81, and 90 percent of the top 10, 25, and 50 features on the synthetic designs, against 0, 3, and 14 percent for CIF and none for random forests, which is consistent with single trees ranking near the bottom of the benchmark.

CIF also uses more distinct noise features than CIF-all, 265 versus 93
on average. For the $p{=}1{,}000$ classification setting with two informative
features, CIF uses informative features in 9.0\% of splits, while CIF-all uses
them in 100.0\%. In regression, CIF-all uses informative features more often
than CIF, 42.3\% versus 27.8\%, but uses more distinct noise features, 528.8
versus 367.7.
CIF-all isolates feature subsampling because feature muting and
the other tree settings are held fixed. In these sparse high-$p$ simulations,
splits use informative features most often when every nonconstant feature can
enter Stage~A. When the sampled feature subset rarely includes an informative
feature at a node, Stage~A selects from that subset.
