\subsection{Where CIF loses: high p and small k}
\label{sec:boundary}

The introduction asks where CIF recovery is weaker, and on real data the
answer has two parts: at small $k$, where
\cref{fig:k-trajectory,fig:regression-k-trajectory} place it 7th or 8th of 17
in classification on the complete-case panel, and on wide datasets, where its
scores peak before the full feature set.
Across the 15 classification datasets with more than 100 features, CIF first
reaches its best score at an intermediate value of $k$ ($100 < k < p$) in 29 of 45
dataset-learner combinations. It first reaches its best score at the full
feature set ($k=p$) in only 7 of 45 combinations.
\Cref{fig:high-p-boundary} shows this pattern by downstream learner.
Classification scores most often peak at
intermediate $k$: 8 of 15 LR datasets, 12 of 15 SVM datasets, and 9 of 15 KNN
datasets. The full feature set is mainly an LR exception. Moving from $k{=}100$
to $k{=}p$ gives a mean $k{=}p$ minus $k{=}100$ score change of $+0.026$ for LR,
and LR first reaches its best score at $k=p$ in 5 of 15 datasets. For SVM and
KNN, the corresponding mean changes are $-0.053$ and $-0.071$, and $k=p$ is
first-best in only 1 of 15 datasets for each learner; the three counts sum to
the 7 of 45 above.

In the 6 high-$p$ regression datasets, full-feature evaluation is rarely best.
Across the 18 dataset-learner combinations, 12 peak at or below $k{=}100$, only
2 peak at $k=p$, and the $k{=}p$ minus $k{=}100$ score change has a mean of
$-0.270$ and a median of $-0.076$.

\subsection{Mechanism: cardinality and feature use}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The paper's central question, whether the multiplicity-corrected split rule
improves a ranking, has a mechanism side: whether the rule changes which kinds
of features a ranking favors, and whether that change improves recovery. The
measurements below address it through cardinality preference in the ranking,
the size of each ranker's support, and the limit that feature sampling places
on which features a forest can split on.

\paragraph{Cardinality in the ranking.}
Two designs, one per task, measure cardinality preference directly in the ranking
(\cref{tab:weak-signal-cardinality}; every ranker and cell is in the reference
release). Ten standard normal features carry a common linear signal whose
marginal correlation with the response is set to 0.05, 0.10, or 0.20, and they
sit among 25 noise columns of integer codes on 500 levels, 25 binary noise
columns, and 10 Gaussian noise columns, with the column order shuffled; each
ranker is scored by the composition of its top ten over 25 fits. CIF here is
the selected benchmark configuration with RDC replaced by MC in classification
and PC in regression, so the measurement concerns the linear selectors, and
the RDC selector of the benchmark configuration was not run on these designs.
The reference for each noise type is the share that a ranker indifferent to
cardinality would give it: 25 of the 60 noise columns are 500-level and 25 are
binary, so each type would receive 25/60 of the top-ten places not taken by
informative features, 0.24 to 0.31 across the four rankers at signal 0.05. The
two biases in question are the preference of impurity importance for features
with more possible thresholds and the reverse cardinality penalty of the
per-threshold Stage~B rule, which lowers the probability of any split on a
feature with many candidate thresholds (\cref{sec:method}). Both appear
against that reference. At the weakest signal random forest impurity
importance fills 0.38 of its top ten with 500-level noise in both tasks,
against a reference of 0.24, and none with binary noise. Per-threshold CIF is
close to the reference in classification (0.31 binary and 0.26 500-level
against 0.29) and leans toward binary noise in regression (0.40 binary and
0.24 500-level against 0.31), and the max-type rule brings the binary share to
0.26 in classification and 0.30 in regression, against references of 0.28 and
0.27.

Reducing the preference does not buy recovery. Random forest impurity
importance places the most informative features in its top ten at every signal
strength (0.42 to 0.43 at 0.05, 0.72 to 0.73 at 0.10, 0.99 at 0.20) against
0.26 to 0.34 for the CIF variants at the weakest signal and 0.64 to 0.66 at
0.10, with standard errors of 0.01 to 0.03, and the downstream scores of all
rankers are within 0.02 of each other at every signal. The same random forest
ranked by out-of-bag permutation importance keeps the preference: its
500-level share at the two weaker signals is 0.47 and 0.36 in classification
and 0.43 and 0.27 in regression, above impurity importance in both tasks. In
classification its recovery is the lowest of the four rankers at every signal;
in regression it is the lowest only at 0.10, exceeds per-threshold CIF at 0.05
(0.37 against 0.26), and ties CIF at 0.20. On these designs, then,
per-threshold conditional inference replaces the impurity preference for
many-valued noise with a smaller preference for binary noise, close to neutral
in classification and 0.09 above the reference in regression, while keeping a
0.22 to 0.28 share of 500-level noise at the weakest signal, and it recovers
fewer weak features than impurity importance. The benefit of a ranking closer
to cardinality-neutral shows on predictors whose cardinality carries no
signal, as in the clinical application of \citet{MilletichDownesHirst2026citrees}, rather than
in recovery of weak marginal effects.

\paragraph{Support.}
A second measurement, on the synthetic benchmark datasets, records each
ranker's support, the features that win at least one split. Random forests
give every feature nonzero importance on 15 of the 16 designs, and 800 of
1,000 on the 1,000-feature classification design. On 13 of the 16 designs
CIF's support keeps 90 to 100 percent of the planted features while excluding
1 to 93 percent of all features (under 5 percent on the two redundant designs,
where the correlated copies all win splits; 41 of 100 features with 9.0 of 10
planted in the standard regression design); on the weak-signal
classification, heteroscedastic regression, and 1,000-feature classification
designs it keeps 41 to 47 percent of the planted features (4.1 of 10 inside a
support of 35 of 100, 4.7 of 10 inside 14 of 50, and 2.1 of 5 inside 91 of
1,000), so the support is a shortlist rather than an estimate of the number
of informative features. Beyond the support, a ranking orders the remaining
zero-importance features by the tie rule of \cref{sec:method}, and these
features are the filler of the ranking. For a single tree the filler is most of any top-$k$ list: CIT
supports hold 0 to 10 features on average, so filler is 52, 81, and 90
percent of the top 10, 25, and 50 features on the synthetic designs, against
0, 3, and 14 percent for CIF and none for random forests, which is consistent
with single trees ranking near the bottom of the benchmark.

\paragraph{Feature use in sampled forests.}
A sparse simulation measures how often forest splits use informative features
when each node samples a subset of features
(\cref{fig:candidate-forest-counts}; \cref{app:candidate-availability} gives
the classification and regression results over $p \in \{100,500,1{,}000\}$).
In the single-tree simulations, CIT uses informative features in 100.0\% of
classification splits and 93.5\% of regression splits. Averaged over the 12
classification forest designs with 1,000 trees, CIF uses informative features
in 33.2\% of splits, compared with 91.7\% for CIF-all, 8.4\% for RF, and 6.5\%
for ET. This measurement counts split usage only, and two qualifications bound
it. The gap between CIF and RF or ET is confounded by tree size: RF and ET grow
to purity, so most of their splits are deep splits on noise, while CIF stops
at the permutation gate. The CIF-all comparison reflects the combinatorics of
feature sampling rather than a property of conditional inference: with 32 of
1,000 features sampled at a node and two informative, an informative feature
is a candidate at 6.3 percent of nodes, and in that design CIF uses one in
9.0\% of splits, RF in 2.7\% and ET in 2.0\%, while CIF-all uses one in
100.0\%. When the sampled subset holds no informative feature, Stage~A selects
from the noise it was given. Evaluating every feature at every node lifts that
floor; the downstream ranking of CIF-all was not scored, so the measurement
identifies the mechanism and leaves its effect on ranking quality open.
