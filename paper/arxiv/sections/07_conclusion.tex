\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The question was whether the split-selection correction of conditional
inference improves a feature ranking used for downstream prediction, and what
it costs. On 31 real datasets CIF is the best conditional inference ranker in the
study, ahead of cforest, ctree, and single CIT, and it ranks tied 3rd of 17 and
2nd of 18 overall, level with random forest rankings, behind recursive feature
elimination and XGBoost and, on the 13-dataset complete-case panel, behind all
three boosting families; with the out-of-bag permutation forest added it is 4th
of 18 in classification, and the max-type configuration recommended below ranks
5th and 4th. The correction trades the high-cardinality preference of
CART-style splitting, which the NHANES application of the companion article
shows on real clinical predictors, for a low-cardinality preference of
comparable size under the per-threshold rule; on these benchmarks it shows in
which features are ranked, with top-$k$ scores level with random forests and
below impurity importance in the recovery of weak signals. Out-of-bag
permutation importance removes the high-cardinality preference from an
ordinary random forest on the clinical cohort but not on the weak-signal
designs, ranks below CIF on the benchmark (8th of 18 and 6th of 19), and its
permutation step grows with the number of features.

The answer to the second part is precise. The cost of the per-threshold
Stage~B rule is its permutation budget, which must grow as $K^2/\alpha$ in the
number of candidate thresholds for the rule to reject at all; the feature test
and its adaptive stopping are not the cost at the benchmark's budget. The
max-type threshold test resolves the same level at $999K$ evaluations, is
calibrated closer to the nominal level, loses about 0.006 in power at matched
size, and moves the downstream rankings by at most two positions, so it is the
configuration a user who wants conditional inference at a practical cost should
choose; its fixed-node guarantee covers a fixed budget and a predictor fixed
without the response, and its calibration under adaptive stopping in fitted
trees rests on the simulations. For a practitioner choosing a ranking method for
downstream prediction, conditional inference delivers a ranking level with
random forests whose split decisions pass a calibrated fixed-node test; its cost
is the threshold test, and the max-type construction is where that budget is
recovered.
