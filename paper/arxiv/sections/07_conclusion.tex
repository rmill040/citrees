\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Conditional inference forests rank features for downstream prediction as well
as random forests, and better than every other conditional inference method. On
31 real datasets CIF ranks tied 3rd of 17 in classification, behind RF-RFE and
XGBoost and level with RF, and 2nd of 18 in regression, behind ExtraTrees. The
result holds under configuration reselection and when CIF and cforest use the
same importance measure, and the standard remedies for the cardinality bias of
random forest importance rank below the forest itself. On the complete-case
panels CIF is tied 6th of 17 and tied 5th of 18, and the regression ordering is
descriptive.

CIF recovers the truly informative features only as well as the middle of the
field, 8th of 15 and 11th of 16 by the average over list sizes, and it is weakest at
small $k$ in classification and on wide data. Its ranking rests on the forest
rather than on the split test. The unbiased split selection that conditional
inference is known for removes the random forest's preference for many-valued
features without improving recovery or prediction.

The split tests set the cost instead. Under the minimum budget the Stage~A
feature test needs of order $m_t^2/\alpha$ permutations per node over $m_t$
features, and the per-threshold rule of order $K^2/\alpha$ over $K$ candidate
thresholds. The opt-in max-type rule keeps the fixed-node guarantee with about
$999K$ statistic evaluations, loses 0.006 in power at equal realized size, and
moves CIF to 5th of 17 and 4th of 18. CIF therefore matches random forests as a
feature ranker while every split it makes passes a permutation test that is valid
at a fixed node, and the max-type rule makes those tests affordable.
